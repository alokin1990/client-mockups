Gum treatment depends on the depth and pattern of inflammation, bone support, buildup below the gums, and how the tissues respond over time.

The diagnosis

Measurements, bleeding, recession, buildup, and imaging help distinguish routine prevention from active periodontal treatment.

What happens next

The exact sequence depends on the examination and the treatment that is appropriate for you.

  3. 03

Detailed guidance

Questions to review before scheduling

How is gum disease evaluated? Which treatments may be discussed? How is progress monitored?

How is gum disease evaluated?

Bleeding, swelling, recession, bad breath, tooth mobility, and other changes can have different causes. A periodontal evaluation may include health and risk review, examination, pocket measurements, and appropriate imaging to understand inflammation, attachment, and bone support.

Which treatments may be discussed?

The plan may include home-care changes, a different cleaning or maintenance schedule, scaling and root planing, or referral and additional periodontal care when needed. Adjunctive medication is case-dependent and should not be presented as routine for every patient.

How is progress monitored?

Follow-up checks bleeding, pocket depth, inflammation, comfort, and home care. Gum disease is associated with some systemic conditions, but association does not prove that periodontal treatment prevents or treats those medical conditions. Coordinate questions with the appropriate healthcare professionals.

Common questions

What patients often ask before scheduling

These answers are general. Your examination and treatment plan determine what applies to you.

Can gum disease be diagnosed from bleeding alone?

No. Bleeding deserves attention, but diagnosis uses an examination, measurements, history, and appropriate imaging.

Is scaling and root planing the same as a routine cleaning?

No. They address different periodontal findings. The dentist should explain the recommended service and maintenance plan.

Does treating gum disease treat heart disease or diabetes?

No. Gum disease is associated with some systemic conditions, but periodontal treatment is not a substitute for medical care.
