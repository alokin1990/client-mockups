What a root canal treats

The problem is inside the tooth

Inside each tooth is soft tissue called pulp. Deep decay, a crack, injury, or repeated dental work can inflame or infect it. Root canal treatment removes that tissue, cleans and seals the canals, and is followed by a restoration that protects the tooth.

Symptoms alone cannot tell you whether this treatment is needed or whether the tooth can be restored. The examination matters.

When to call promptly: Contact the office if pain or swelling is increasing. For trouble breathing or swallowing, uncontrolled bleeding, major facial or jaw trauma, or spreading facial swelling, call 911 or go to an emergency department.

Lingering sensitivity

Hot or cold that continues to bother a tooth after the stimulus is gone can be one reason to arrange an examination.

Pain when biting

Chewing pain may come from several causes, including a crack or an issue inside the tooth. Testing is needed to distinguish them.

Swelling or a damaged tooth

Swollen gum tissue, deep decay, trauma, or a cracked tooth should be assessed rather than treated as a certain root canal diagnosis.

Keeping and restoring a tooth

The canal is only part of the plan

When a tooth is restorable, root canal care aims to keep it in place. The dentist also considers how much sound tooth remains, the bite, and what final restoration can protect it.

When the tooth can be restored

A filling or crown may be recommended after the canal is sealed. The choice depends on tooth location, remaining structure, and the forces placed on it.

When the tooth cannot be restored

Extraction or referral may be discussed. If a tooth must be removed, dental implants are one possible replacement topic; they are not the right option for everyone.

Treatment does not guarantee that a tooth will remain problem-free. New decay, fracture, or persistent symptoms may call for further evaluation.

What happens at the visit

From examination to final restoration

The details depend on the tooth and provider. This is the usual sequence to discuss, not a promise about the number of appointments or a specific procedure.

  1. Examine and diagnose

    The dentist reviews symptoms and dental history, examines the tooth and bite, and may take radiographs to see the roots and nearby structures.

  2. Agree on the plan

    Ask about restorability, alternatives, whether a specialist should treat the tooth, the expected appointment sequence, and the estimate.

  3. Treat the canal when appropriate

    Root canal care generally involves removing inflamed or infected pulp, cleaning and shaping the canals, then filling and sealing them. Your clinician should explain the steps planned for your tooth.

  4. Protect and review the tooth

    A temporary or final restoration follows according to the plan. A filling or crown may be needed, and follow-up is part of care. Contact the treating office if swelling or pain worsens.

Root canal FAQs

Questions to bring to your evaluation

An examination gives you an answer based on your tooth rather than symptoms alone.

How will the dentist know if I need a root canal?

The dentist considers symptoms, tooth history, examination findings, and imaging when needed. Other causes of pain may lead to a different plan.

Will I need a crown afterward?

Some treated teeth need a crown; others may be restored with a filling. The dentist will consider tooth location, remaining structure, and bite before recommending a final restoration.

What if the tooth had a root canal before?

New symptoms in a previously treated tooth call for another examination. Depending on the findings, retreatment, a different procedure, extraction, or specialist referral may be discussed.

How many visits will care take?

The number of visits depends on diagnosis, complexity, provider, and whether a final restoration is placed at a separate appointment. The team can explain the sequence after evaluating your tooth.
